# DreamScriptures individual dream-page editorial standard

## Core principle

Every indexed page must help a reader understand a specific dream. A familiar layout is useful; interchangeable reasoning is not. A page should be shorter when additional sections would only repeat the same idea.

## The 2 a.m. reader test

Before approving any page, ask:

> If a real person searched this exact dream at 2 a.m. because the dream genuinely bothered or intrigued them, would this page make them feel understood—and would it give them something meaningful they could not get from a generic dream dictionary?

The reviewer must answer both parts:

1. **Would the reader feel understood?** Identify the sentence, distinction, or question that recognizes the emotional reality of this particular dream.
2. **What would they gain here that is not generic?** Name the page-specific reasoning involving behavior, setting, emotion, ending, relationship, or waking context.

If either answer is vague, the page is not ready. A high structural score cannot overrule this test.

## Non-negotiable publishing gates

A page cannot be approved while any of these are true:

1. The opening delays the answer with history, mystery, or generic remarks about dreams.
2. The central interpretation could be pasted onto several unrelated dream pages without meaningful changes.
3. Emotional, symbolic, spiritual, or practical sections repeat the same claim in different words.
4. A warning, spiritual claim, health concern, death, pregnancy, betrayal, curse, or future event is presented as certain.
5. Related links lead to missing pages or have no defensible relationship to the current dream.
6. The page exists mainly to capture a keyword and has no distinct search intent or interpretation.
7. The page is difficult to scan or understand on a narrow mobile screen.

## Human-review rubric

Score each category from 0 to 2.

- **0 — Fails:** absent, misleading, generic, or substantially repeated.
- **1 — Serviceable:** useful but incomplete, broad, or in need of a more specific example.
- **2 — Strong:** specific, clear, nuanced, and difficult to reuse on another page.

### 1. Direct answer

- The first meaningful paragraph answers “What might this dream mean?”
- It names a small number of plausible themes rather than a long keyword list.
- It identifies the dream detail or emotion that changes the reading.

### 2. Core interpretation

- The page has one clear central idea tied to this exact event, symbol/image, or relationship.
- It answers the likely intent behind the exact search phrase, not merely the broad keyword.

### 3. Emotional reasoning

- The emotional section explains what fear, peace, shame, anger, relief, curiosity, or another relevant feeling changes.
- It does not simply rename the symbol as an emotion.

### 4. Symbolic reasoning

- The symbolic section examines behavior, condition, setting, movement, or relationship to the dreamer.
- It avoids declaring one universal definition.

### 5. Spiritual and biblical care

- Spiritual or biblical material appears only when it adds something supported and relevant.
- Scripture is used in context.
- Possibility, discernment, and uncertainty remain visible.
- A missing spiritual or biblical section is acceptable when it would add no honest value.

### 6. Waking-life usefulness

- The practical section connects the dream with recognizable situations, choices, habits, memories, or recent experiences.
- It leaves room for ordinary dream formation, stress, media exposure, bodily sensations, and memory processing.

### 7. Conditional depth

- Common variations produce meaningfully different readings.
- At least two of these are considered where relevant: behavior, setting, emotion, other people, ending, condition, or dreamer response.

### 8. Reflection questions

- Questions could not be moved unchanged to an unrelated page.
- They help the reader distinguish between plausible meanings rather than merely asking for engagement.

### 9. Contextual pathways

- Each related dream has a real destination and a reason for the relationship.
- Connections can be symbolic, emotional, event-based, opposing, spiritual, or grounded in a shared waking concern.
- Dream Compass is introduced as a way to explore the reader’s version of the dream.

### 10. Voice and mobile readability

- The tone is warm, calm, exploratory, and direct.
- Paragraphs remain readable on mobile and headings reveal what the next section contributes.
- The page avoids fear, inflated importance, repetitive conclusions, and mechanical transitions.

## Approval rule

A page is ready to serve as an editorial benchmark when:

- It scores at least **17 out of 20**.
- Direct answer, core interpretation, safety, and contextual pathways receive no score of 0.
- The reviewer can state in one sentence why the page deserves to exist separately.
- The reviewer records a specific answer to both parts of the 2 a.m. reader test.
- All links resolve and the page passes mobile, keyboard, and encoding checks.

Pages scoring 13–16 remain useful but need a focused revision. Pages scoring 9–12 require substantial editorial work. A page below 9 should be considered for merging or noindexing before expansion.

## What an excellent page looks like

An excellent page moves in this order:

1. A concise answer tied to the exact dream.
2. The key variable that changes the meaning.
3. Distinct emotional and symbolic reasoning.
4. Spiritual or biblical reflection only when it earns its place.
5. Conditional variations based on what happened.
6. Practical possibilities, including ordinary sources of dream material.
7. Specific questions that help the reader compare interpretations.
8. Related pages with visible reasons.
9. A calm transition into Dream Compass.

The page never claims to know more than the dream supports. It gives the reader useful possibilities, shows the reasoning behind them, and leaves room for the reader’s own context.

## Review workflow

1. Read the page without looking at its keyword or SEO fields.
2. Write the one-sentence reason the page deserves to exist.
3. Record why a worried or curious late-night reader would feel understood.
4. Record what useful insight the page offers beyond a generic dictionary definition.
5. Score the ten rubric categories.
6. Mark repeated claims across lenses.
7. Check every scenario, question, related reason, source, and link.
8. Read the first 30% on a mobile viewport.
9. Decide: approve, revise, merge, or keep accessible with noindex.
10. Record the reviewer, date, decision, and the most important remaining weakness.

## Phase 3.5 calibration gates

The six-page calibration added four checks that apply before a repaired page can become a category model:

1. **Inherited-content gate:** Read the rendered page all the way through. An authored override does not pass if older “A closer look” or taxonomy prose still dilutes the page’s specific anchor.
2. **Anchor-placement gate:** State the one-sentence interpretive anchor in the answer or first core paragraph. A strong idea that appears only in a later lens is not enough for the first-screen reader.
3. **Detail-reason gate:** For every major scenario, explain why that detail changes the interpretation. Lists of associations without a change in meaning remain generic.
4. **Neighbor-visibility gate:** When a close semantic neighbor exists, tell the reader what that neighbor adds and why this page remains distinct. Technical link relevance alone is insufficient.

These are calibration gates, not a requirement that every page use the same structure, length, or number of scenarios.

## Phase 4 inherited-content rule

Inherited-content review must be performed on the rendered route, not only on the enrichment object. Mark each inherited section **Keep**, **Adapt**, **Replace**, or **Remove**, then re-read the first 40% after composition. A page does not pass because a strong override exists if the renderer still places generic base prose before or after it.

For relationship neighbors that reverse the subject/object of a fear (for example, confessing versus receiving or cheating versus being cheated on), the distinction must appear in the opening or first scenario, not only in metadata or a related-dream card.
