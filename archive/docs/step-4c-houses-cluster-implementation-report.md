# Step 4C — Houses Dreams Topic Cluster

## Executive summary

The Houses cluster is implemented around one organizing destination, `/guides/house-dreams`, and 27 existing individual dream pages. No new individual dream pages, indexing directives, canonicals, redirects, or URLs were created or changed. The hub teaches readers to interpret familiarity, condition, rooms, access, privacy, ownership, movement, and the people present instead of treating a house as a fixed symbol.

Three strategically central dependencies were repaired: `old-house`, `empty-house`, and the canonical `being-lost-in-a-building` page. The legacy `lost-in-a-building` URL remains a redirect alias to that canonical page and is not listed as a separate cluster destination.

## Architecture

### Existing inventory

The inventory covered the `Houses and places` category and house/home/place pages involving current, new, old, childhood, empty, haunted, damaged, burning, flooded, unfinished, hidden-room, basement, doors, stairs, crowding, break-ins, and navigation experiences. Existing supporting pathways include the broad house page, loss-of-control guidance, nostalgia/feeling-trapped emotional pathways, and the general symbol-interpretation guide. Adjacent school, hospital, church, forest, court, and animal-in-house pages were not pulled into the core merely because they describe a place or happen to contain “house” in a related phrase.

### Hub decision and purpose

No suitable dedicated Houses hub existed. One hub was added at `/guides/house-dreams`. Its unique purpose is to help readers identify whether the meaningful part of a house dream is the space itself, its condition, the access it allows, the history it carries, or what happened inside it—something a single symbol page cannot explain efficiently.

### Retained core pages

The hub organizes 27 existing pages into four pathways:

- Familiarity, belonging, and change: `house`, `new-house`, `big-house`, `old-house`, `moving-back-to-an-old-house`, `moving-to-a-new-house`, `childhood-home`, `house-in-the-middle-of-nowhere`, `empty-house`, `haunted-house`.
- Condition and what the structure can hold: `house-collapsing`, `house-burning`, `house-on-fire`, `mud-house`, `flooded-house`, `unfinished-building`.
- Rooms, privacy, and discovery: `discovering-new-rooms-in-a-house`, `secret-room`, `basement`, `being-in-a-small-crowded-room`.
- Access, movement, and boundaries: `many-doors`, `climbing-stairs`, `going-down-stairs`, `room-closing-in-on-you`, `house-break-in-while-sleeping`, `being-lost-in-a-building`, `being-lost-in-your-own-house`.

`lost-in-a-building` is a maintained redirect alias, not a second intent. The near-duplicate is therefore represented once through `being-lost-in-a-building`. Snake-in-house, school, hospital, church, and other setting-specific pages remain owned by their stronger existing topic or experience rather than being added to this hub.

### Semantic overlaps and boundaries

- House versus room: a whole house can concern belonging, ownership, family, safety, or life structure; one room localizes the question to function, memory, privacy, concealment, or discovery.
- `old-house` versus `moving-back-to-an-old-house`: the former interprets age, condition, history, and relationship to the structure; the latter centers the decision or act of returning.
- `new-house` versus `moving-to-a-new-house`: the former focuses on the destination and what it offers; the latter focuses on transition, relocation, and leaving one structure for another.
- `secret-room` versus `discovering-new-rooms-in-a-house`: the former centers what is hidden; the latter centers the discovery process and the dreamer’s response to finding it.
- `being-lost-in-a-building` versus `being-lost-in-your-own-house`: the canonical building page focuses on navigation through an institution or complex public structure; the familiar-home page focuses on identity, belonging, memory, and access in a supposedly known place.
- `house-burning` versus `house-on-fire`: both remain available for now, but their overlap is a future consolidation-review item. No merge was executed in this phase.

The hub makes the house/room distinction explicit and uses contextual reasons on pathways rather than keyword-only link labels.

## Dependency repairs

### `old-house`

- Previous status: P1; pre-repair score recorded as 24 in the dependency-selection inventory.
- Current status: KEEP; current audit score 22, tier P1. The lower numerical score reflects the stricter current audit rubric, not a regression in the rendered page.
- Anchor: age is interpreted through relationship, condition, access, and the past the house carries—not automatically through nostalgia.
- Changes: direct opening, contextual emotional/symbolic/spiritual/practical interpretation, four detail-to-context scenarios, five specific reflection questions, contextual related links, and a natural general-to-personal Dream Compass route.
- Inherited handling: generic age-as-the-past framing was adapted; useful distinctions about memory, inherited expectations, repair, and neglect were kept; broad dictionary language was replaced.
- Neighbor distinction: returning to the house is directed to `moving-back-to-an-old-house`; childhood-specific memory is directed to `childhood-home`.

### `empty-house`

- Previous status: P1 in the dependency inventory.
- Current status: KEEP; current audit tier P2.
- Anchor: absence changes agency, privacy, belonging, and responsibility; emptiness is not synonymous with loneliness.
- Changes: opening answer, emotion-dependent readings (peace, sadness, fear, curiosity), ownership and expected-occupant distinctions, four scenarios, five reflection questions, contextual links, and Dream Compass transition.
- Inherited handling: generic loneliness framing was replaced; material about autonomy, grief, privacy, and changed roles was retained or adapted.
- Neighbor distinction: `house` handles belonging/ownership broadly, while `house-break-in-while-sleeping` provides the contrasting unwanted-presence and boundary experience.

### `being-lost-in-a-building`

- Previous status: P1 dependency candidate; the public `lost-in-a-building` path is an alias redirect.
- Current status: KEEP; current audit tier P2.
- Anchor: navigation inside a complex institution or structured public space when the building should provide routes but does not.
- Changes: direct opening, building-type distinctions, route/level/access reasoning, four scenarios, five reflection questions, contextual links, and Dream Compass transition.
- Inherited handling: generic “a building represents your life” material was replaced with institution, role, rule, sign, corridor, and access reasoning; ordinary wayfinding and recent-building exposure remain acknowledged.
- Neighbor distinction: the page is intentionally separate from being lost in one’s own house, where familiarity and identity—not institutional navigation—are central.

## Hub structure and reader journey

The hub opens with the broad answer and interpretive principles, then groups existing pages by familiarity, condition, rooms/privacy, and access/boundaries. It includes emotional patterns, reflection questions, a clearly bounded perspective note, related pathways, and one Dream Compass CTA. Discovery modules were restrained so the hub does not compete with itself through multiple “related reading” systems.

The intended journey is: search result → specific house experience → Houses hub for context → one relevant neighboring interpretation → Dream Compass for personal exploration.

## Editorial findings

Recurring Houses problems were generic “house equals self” definitions, condition lists without reasoning, and navigation links based only on shared place vocabulary. The most useful repairs connected the physical detail to access, ownership, privacy, familiarity, and the dreamer’s action. House pages also frequently collapsed rooms into the whole structure; the hub now teaches why a room can localize an experience without pretending that every room has a universal meaning.

Trust-sensitive language avoids diagnosing trauma, assuming abuse, or treating haunted/break-in imagery as proof of danger. Ordinary explanations—recent moves, viewed properties, family conversations, spatial memory, and building layouts—remain available alongside psychological, cultural, and spiritual possibilities.
