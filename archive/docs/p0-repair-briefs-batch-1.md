# P0 editorial repair briefs — Batch 1

This pilot covers three pages only. The briefs were completed before their content was changed.

## Being Lost

**Primary search intent:** Understand why the dreamer could not find a place, person, route, or destination—and whether the dream reflects confusion, a decision, belonging, or a transition.

**What the current page does well:** It notices that location, asking for help, and eventually finding the way can change the meaning. Its related links already follow uncertainty and navigation.

**Where it fails the 2 a.m. reader test:** The page repeats “uncertainty, confusion, transition, and self-discovery” without closely examining what the dreamer was trying to reach or why being unable to arrive felt important.

**What the reader is probably trying to understand:** Whether they are truly directionless, afraid of making the wrong choice, separated from someone important, or adjusting to a place or role that no longer feels familiar.

**Missing emotional/contextual insight:** Panic, calm exploration, shame about needing help, urgency, and relief after finding a route need separate interpretations.

**Missing unique interpretation:** Being lost can reveal a conflict between destination and method: the dreamer may know what matters but not how to reach it, or may be following a route that no longer belongs to them.

**Closest competing page:** `lost-in-a-building`.

**How this page should be meaningfully different:** This page interprets the broad experience of lostness across settings, destinations, social separation, and outcomes. The neighbor should remain focused on enclosed architecture, confusing rooms, institutions, and internal structure.

**Sections that should be rewritten:** Opening, core description, emotional meaning, symbolic meaning, waking-life meaning, scenarios, and reflection questions.

**Sections that should remain:** The cautious spiritual premise, the useful support-oriented related links, and the distinction between missing information and difficulty committing.

**Reflection questions needed:** What was the destination? Was the dreamer alone? Did familiar landmarks change? Was help available? Did the dream end in arrival, acceptance, or continued searching?

**Internal-link opportunities:** Being in an Unknown Place, Being Trapped in a Maze, Lost in a Building, and Travel—each for a different aspect of uncertainty.

**Dream Compass opportunity:** Invite the reader to explore the exact setting, destination, companions, emotion, and outcome rather than treating “lost” as a single symbol.

**Unique editorial anchor:** The dream may not be saying “you have no direction.” It may reveal where a meaningful destination has become disconnected from a workable route.

## Being Stuck in an Elevator

**Primary search intent:** Understand the trapped, stalled, or falling sensation and whether it relates to progress, status, confinement, or dependence on forces outside the dreamer’s control.

**What the current page does well:** Its opening clearly distinguishes elevators from stairs and recognizes that expected movement has become suspension. It also avoids treating delay as supernatural obstruction.

**Where it fails the 2 a.m. reader test:** It recognizes the scene but does not fully interpret differences between panic, calm waiting, calling for help, forced escape, upward intention, downward travel, and sudden falling.

**What the reader is probably trying to understand:** Why progress stopped, why they could not leave, and whether the dream reflects being trapped or simply being forced to wait.

**Missing emotional/contextual insight:** Claustrophobia differs from impatience; embarrassment about needing rescue differs from fear of falling; calmness can indicate trust rather than passivity.

**Missing unique interpretation:** An elevator represents movement delegated to a system. When it fails, the dream can expose the difference between personal effort and progress controlled by an employer, institution, relationship, timetable, or gatekeeper.

**Closest competing page:** `running-but-cant-move-fast` in the audit; `being-trapped` is the closest conceptual link.

**How this page should be meaningfully different:** Running slowly concerns effort that produces too little movement. The elevator page concerns movement entrusted to a mechanism or authority that stops responding.

**Sections that should be rewritten:** Emotional, symbolic, practical, scenarios, reflection questions, and related pathways.

**Sections that should remain:** Opening, core elevator-versus-stairs distinction, spiritual caution, biblical qualification, and accident reassurance.

**Reflection questions needed:** Intended floor, who controlled the elevator, emotional response to asking for help, whether doors opened, and whether the dreamer waited or escaped.

**Internal-link opportunities:** Being Trapped, Climbing Stairs, Falling, and Running but Can’t Move Fast.

**Dream Compass opportunity:** Use direction, floor, motion, companions, and outcome to distinguish stalled progress from fear of losing status or control.

**Unique editorial anchor:** The dream is often about progress that has been delegated to something outside the dreamer—not merely feeling trapped.

## Unable to Call or Communicate

**Primary search intent:** Understand why an urgent call, text, warning, request for help, or attempt to be heard repeatedly failed.

**What the current page does well:** It vividly recognizes wrong numbers, missing signal, unsent messages, and a voice that cannot be heard. It responsibly warns that the dream is not an instruction to contact someone.

**Where it fails the 2 a.m. reader test:** The page identifies communication failure but needs to distinguish access, expression, reception, urgency, and relational safety more clearly.

**What the reader is probably trying to understand:** Whether they miss someone, fear being unsupported, need to say something, or feel that speaking will not change the outcome.

**Missing emotional/contextual insight:** Panic about an emergency is different from sadness over distance, anger at being ignored, or relief that a message never sent.

**Missing unique interpretation:** The failed part of the communication chain matters. Forgetting a number concerns access or memory; losing one’s voice concerns expression or safety; not being heard concerns reception; repeatedly editing an unsent text concerns ambivalence about consequences.

**Closest competing page:** `phone-not-working`.

**How this page should be meaningfully different:** Phone Not Working centers on malfunctioning technology and disrupted access. This page centers on the human message, the intended recipient, and the point at which connection breaks—even when no phone is involved.

**Sections that should be rewritten:** Emotional, symbolic, practical, scenarios, reflection questions, and related pathways.

**Sections that should remain:** Opening, concrete malfunction details, spiritual restraint, biblical listening theme, and boundary-aware FAQ.

**Reflection questions needed:** Intended recipient, exact message, urgency, failure point, emotional response, and whether silence felt frustrating or protective.

**Internal-link opportunities:** Phone Not Working, Being Unable to Speak, and Being Trapped for emergency helplessness.

**Dream Compass opportunity:** Let the reader identify who they needed, what they hoped would happen after contact, and whether the obstacle was access, expression, or being heard.

**Unique editorial anchor:** The dream’s meaning lies less in the phone than in where the path from inner need to shared understanding breaks down.
