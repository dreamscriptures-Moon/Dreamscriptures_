## Editorial findings

### Recurring generic patterns

- Theme stacks such as growth, transformation, hope, guidance, and uncertainty appeared before the dream's actual mechanism.
- Legacy symbolic sections repeatedly defined the noun rather than explaining the event.
- Related links were often based on “growth,” “journey,” or “spiritual awareness” instead of a reader's next question.

### Inherited-content patterns

- Strong ideas often existed in one sentence—ending after a fall, unfamiliar but not lost, a building not ready for use—and were diluted by long abstract tails.
- Short fragments and symmetrical lists created an AI/SEO rhythm even where the central observation was useful.
- The repair retained those strong ideas and removed or replaced the surrounding generalization.

### Category and trust findings

- Body/family pages require ordinary bodily, reproductive, medical, grief, and media context without diagnosis or prediction.
- Spiritual pages improved when tradition, possible personal meaning, and evidentiary limits appeared together rather than as an appended disclaimer.
- Environmental imagery needs source and physical conditions before omen language.
- Message dreams need credibility tests: exact content, source identity, behavior, and evidence.

### What improved reader value

- Giving each page a relationship or process anchor.
- Making scenarios explain why a changed detail matters.
- Replacing keyword-related links with explicit broader/specific/contrast paths.
- Adding five questions derived from each page instead of a shared fallback set.
- Preserving useful observations while removing generic tails.

### What mainly changed scores

- Length and detector vocabulary drove most numerical movement. The batch reduced P1 by one overall, but several individually improved pages scored lower because concise, non-repetitive lenses replaced longer inherited copy.
- Score movement was not used to pad or reverse a human editorial decision.

## Whole-page quality gate
