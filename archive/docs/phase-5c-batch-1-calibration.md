## Editorial anchors and interventions

- **Fighting an Angel:** resistance to guidance or authority that appears good but feels imposed. Replaced poetic fragments; added faith-aware uncertainty, behavior scenarios, and questions about what was being protected.
- **Clear Water:** clarity reveals something without guaranteeing safety. Replaced purity/peace shorthand; added depth, current, temperature, distance, and unwanted clarity scenarios.
- **Many Doors:** the problem is choosing among paths, not simply crossing a threshold. Replaced broad choice lists; added sameness, access, time pressure, and reversible-step reasoning.
- **Room with Many People:** crowd meaning depends on social visibility and available personal space. Replaced “humans are social” filler; added role, recognition, exit, and belonging distinctions.
- **Hearing Bells:** a bell is an attention event whose source, rhythm, and response determine whether it signals timing, transition, invitation, or interruption. Replaced “bells mean awareness” shorthand and added specific questions.
- **Intimacy with a Strange Creature:** unfamiliar intimacy tests agency and the dreamer’s relationship with a quality that seems strange. Added consent, creature qualities, shame, and nonliteral framing.
- **Famine:** prolonged scarcity changes how a community survives. Added resource type, distribution, sharing/hoarding, real-world exposure, and restrained biblical context.
- **Being Late for Class:** lateness becomes a test of readiness and belonging within a learning/evaluation setting. Added delay cause, subject, teacher, materials, and whose timetable is being followed.

## Reflection and conditional coverage

All eight pages now contain questions derived from their anchors. The questions ask the reader to reason about source, response, consequence, agency, or context rather than merely recall scene details. Each page also has conditional scenarios; the variables differ by dream instead of following a fixed count.

## Inherited-content results

The main inherited failures were short fragment chains, broad symbolism lists, and generic “this commonly appears during…” paragraphs. These were replaced where they preceded or repeated the anchor. Useful scenario foundations and related-link destinations were retained and rewritten around the page’s specific question.

## What produced genuine reader improvement

1. Replacing fragments with a concrete tension (good authority resisted; clear water that may still be dangerous) improved recognition immediately.
2. Making the setting do interpretive work prevented broad symbols from swallowing the exact search intent.
3. Adding “what were you protecting / what did you do / what changed?” questions improved usefulness more than adding extra definitions.
4. Neighbor distinctions worked when stated as a difference in the reader’s problem, not merely a link title.

## What mainly affected rubric scores

Longer descriptions and additional scenarios raised structural coverage, but did not by themselves improve the reader experience. Many Doors remained P1 because its broad intent naturally collides with Doors; this is not a reason to pad the page or merge it automatically.

## Methodology findings

- Reflection questions are most effective inside the page repair when they express the anchor. A later universal question pass should only address pages that genuinely lack them.
- We found no new inherited-content pattern requiring a new global gate. The existing inherited-content, anchor-placement, detail-reason, neighbor-visibility, and composition-as-authorship gates remain sufficient.
- Broad-symbol pages (Water, Clear Water, Many Doors) need stronger first-screen neighbor distinctions than event-specific pages.
- Sensitive spiritual and intimacy pages require trust boundaries integrated into the interpretation, not appended as generic disclaimers.
