## 1. 11:11 — A (model-ready)

### 2 a.m. reader test

**Pass.** The opening recognizes why a small clock detail can feel charged: “**11:11 in a dream may feel meaningful because repeating numbers naturally catch attention**.” It then gives the reader a usable next question—what the number meant personally, what happened around it, and whether the feeling was curiosity, comfort, pressure, or uncertainty. That is more reassuring than either dismissing the experience or declaring it a sign.

### Generic-dictionary test

**Clearly no, with one inherited risk.** The core cannot be pasted onto another number page without losing its central distinction: “**The surrounding event and your association with 11:11 are more informative than the digits in isolation.**” The page also says spiritual and numerological meanings are traditions, “**not established facts**,” and that the dream does not prove manifestation, an unseen instruction, or a promised outcome. The generic phrase “repeating numbers can mean alignment/awakening” appears only as a clearly labeled tradition, not as the conclusion.

### Anchor

**This page’s unique interpretive anchor is:** 11:11 is an attention-and-association marker; the dream can show what the dreamer is waiting for or trying to make certain, but it cannot establish a supernatural prediction.

### Why the details matter

- A clock makes the detail about timing, waiting, or a deadline; it does not make the moment destined.
- Someone pointing it out shifts the focus to shared attention or pressure to accept another person’s meaning.
- Repeatedly checking the time changes the reading toward reassurance-seeking or anxiety.
- Appearance beside a decision marks emotional importance while leaving practical evidence in charge.

### Neighbor distinction and action

The page remains independent from `angel-numbers` because it treats 11:11 as a personally loaded dream event, while the neighbor covers the broader tradition. No repair is required for approval; the wording should be preserved as a numerology model.

## 2. Crows — B (targeted revision)

### 2 a.m. reader test

**Partial pass.** The answer paragraph is excellent: “**A crow in a dream is not automatically a bad omen. It often acts as an attention director: watching, calling, gathering, taking, or entering a private space.**” A reader would feel protected from the frightening folklore and invited to examine what actually happened. However, the rendered “A closer look” then opens with broad lines such as “**Crows are rarely passive dream symbols**” and “**At its core, the crow often symbolizes awareness**.” Those passages could fit many bird pages and dilute the sharper anchor.

### Generic-dictionary test

**Partially / mostly yes for the inherited middle.** “Awareness,” “mystery,” “hidden,” and “something important beneath the surface” are generic associations when not tied to a crow’s action. The later authored symbolism is much stronger: watching, calling, carrying, entering the home, number, and setting all change the reading.

### Anchor

**This page’s unique interpretive anchor is:** The crow directs attention, and its behavior—not its dark color or folklore—shows whether the dream concerns observation, social pressure, adaptation, loss, or curiosity.

### Why the details matter

- One quiet crow narrows attention to one observer or concern; a loud flock introduces social pressure or competing signals.
- Entering the house moves an outside concern into private life.
- Bringing or taking an object gives the object, possession, or perceived loss the interpretive weight.
- Feeding the crow changes the dreamer from threatened observer to willing participant.

### Neighbor distinction and action

The links to `owl`, `being-watched`, `darkness`, and `snake` have defensible reasons. The targeted repair is to replace the inherited “A closer look” paragraphs with the behavior-first description already present in the symbolic and scenario sections, so the anchor appears before generic awareness language. Until that is done, this is useful but not model-ready.

## 3. Tsunami — A (model-ready)

### 2 a.m. reader test

**Pass.** The opening immediately answers the fear behind the search: “**A tsunami in a dream does not predict a disaster. Its scale can show how large a pressure feels compared with your available coping resources**.” The next clause recognizes distinct experiences—watching, protecting someone, finding high ground, rebuilding—so the reader is not reduced to “overwhelmed.”

### Generic-dictionary test

**Clearly no.** “The wave is not simply water; it is a moving environment that changes every plan around it” is specific to tsunami scale. The page asks what the dreamer did before impact and what survival or aftermath felt like, then grounds the image in news, coastal experience, weather fear, or disaster exposure.

### Anchor

**This page’s unique interpretive anchor is:** Tsunami meaning is a resource-and-response sequence: perceived scale shows the pressure, available support shows capacity, the response shows agency or helplessness, and the outcome shows recovery rather than prediction.

### Why the details matter

- Distance describes anticipation; being inside the water describes demands already exceeding familiar coping methods.
- Warning and evacuation reveal preparation and access to support; freezing or waiting for someone else reveals constrained agency.
- Rescue adds responsibility for another person; survival does not erase grief or shock.
- The aftermath shifts the page from imminent threat to rebuilding and what cannot return exactly as before.

### Neighbor distinction and action

`flood` is a meaningful neighbor but not a duplicate: flood concerns water entering a bounded place, while tsunami changes the whole environment through scale and momentum. Keep this page as the natural-disaster model.

## 4. Marrying a Celebrity — A (model-ready)

### 2 a.m. reader test

**Pass.** The opening names the likely concern—“**what that person represents**”—and recognizes the vulnerable question of “**whether you belong beside an ideal**.” The reader is not told they will date the celebrity; they are helped to examine admiration, visibility, and worth.

### Generic-dictionary test

**Clearly no.** The page explains why marriage changes celebrity symbolism: it brings a distant public quality into an intimate promise. Feeling at home, out of place, publicly displayed, or privately ordinary each produces a different reading. The explicit statement that the celebrity is not thinking about the dreamer and no relationship is predicted is an important trust boundary.

### Anchor

**This page’s unique interpretive anchor is:** The celebrity represents an admired quality or public identity, while marriage tests whether that ideal feels integrated, performative, inaccessible, or genuinely compatible with the dreamer’s ordinary self.

### Why the details matter

- Feeling at home suggests integration; feeling unworthy exposes comparison or impostor fear.
- A public ceremony makes visibility and judgment central; a private wedding makes identity integration more important than applause.
- The celebrity’s profession or public quality gives the ideal its content (creative, influential, beautiful, free, confident).
- A mismatch between public and private self invites the dreamer to question idealization rather than chase an image.

### Neighbor distinction and action

The page clearly separates `being-admired` (attention), `marrying-a-crush` (personal attraction), and `marrying-a-stranger` (unknown identity). It is suitable as the romantic/identity model.

## 5. Eyes — B (targeted revision)

### 2 a.m. reader test

**Pass with a navigation caveat.** “**Eyes in a dream change meaning with the direction of attention**” is a memorable recognition sentence. The page also distinguishes clear vision, closed eyes, reflection, and being watched, and acknowledges physical sensations and medical concerns. A reader with a frightening gaze dream may nevertheless encounter the dedicated dark-watcher page before fully understanding that the broad page is about attention direction.

### Generic-dictionary test

**Partially.** “Eyes symbolize awareness/seeing truth” would be generic, but the authored page moves beyond it by assigning meaning to ownership, gaze direction, eye condition, and whether contact feels caring or intrusive. The remaining risk is that the inherited taxonomy after the core repeats broad “visibility/insight” language.

### Anchor

**This page’s unique interpretive anchor is:** Eyes show who owns attention and where it is directed—outward observation, self-recognition, avoidance, or being watched—with condition and emotion deciding whether the experience is insight, exposure, rest, or impaired trust in one’s judgment.

### Why the details matter

- Clear vision can be relief or unwanted clarity; the emotional result changes the meaning.
- Injury or inability to open the eyes introduces impaired judgment, painful information, reduced control, or a physical sleep sensation.
- A direct gaze can be intimacy, recognition, challenge, or judgment depending on the relationship and consent.
- Many eyes widen the dream toward social evaluation; eyes in darkness narrow it to an unseen watcher.

### Neighbor distinction and action

`eyes-staring-at-you-in-the-dark` is a genuine narrow intent, not a duplicate. Move the explicit “broad eyes vs unseen watcher in darkness” distinction into the first core paragraph and trim any inherited broad-awareness repetition. Then the page can serve as the body/attention model.

## 6. Saying No — A (model-ready)

### 2 a.m. reader test

**Pass.** The opening recognizes the dilemma immediately: “**the boundary between your genuine choice and the pressure to keep everyone else comfortable**.” It asks what the refusal is protecting, then follows with concrete requests (marriage, work, family help) and consequences (space, conflict, punishment, honesty).

### Generic-dictionary test

**Clearly no.** “Set better boundaries” would be generic, but this page distinguishes a wise, avoidant, protective, angry, or uncertain refusal. Its symbolic lens names the claim on time, body, loyalty, identity, money, or future, and the scenarios change when the dreamer cannot speak, feels safer but guilty, or faces anger.

### Anchor

**This page’s unique interpretive anchor is:** A dream refusal is meaningful through what the request would have cost and what the emotional aftermath reveals about consent, pressure, self-trust, and the boundary’s purpose.

### Why the details matter

- Refusing a proposal, extra work, or family help changes the threatened value: readiness, capacity, or independence.
- A confident no rehearses self-trust; an impossible no points toward conflict, dependence, or fear.
- Anger from the requester dramatizes fear of punishment but does not prove the boundary is wrong.
- Relief with guilt shows that care for others and care for self have become entangled.

### Neighbor distinction and action

`saying-yes-when-you-dont-want-to`, `forced-marriage`, and `being-unable-to-speak` each cover a different failure point: suppressed agreement, coercive commitment, and inability to express the boundary. `saying-no-at-a-wedding` should remain a scenario-specific page only if its wedding setting adds independent meaning; the broad page itself is still model-ready.

## Common weaknesses found

1. **Inherited generic prose can survive a successful repair.** Crows is the clearest case: the answer and scenarios are specific, but an older middle section still sounds like a bird-symbol dictionary.
2. **A strong anchor is not enough if it appears late.** Eyes and Crows show that the first 30–40% must carry the distinction, not merely the later lens.
3. **Neighbor distinctions need to be reader-visible.** A related link can be technically justified while still making the broad page feel interchangeable unless the page says what the narrower page adds.
4. **“Why this detail matters” is the quality signal.** The best pages explain a causal or interpretive shift (scale changes resources; ceremony changes visibility; a request changes what is protected), rather than listing associations.

## Editorial-standard changes

The existing standard is sound, but calibration adds four gates:

1. **Inherited-content gate:** Read the rendered page, including base “A closer look” prose and taxonomy sections. A repaired override does not pass if an older generic paragraph dilutes the anchor.
2. **Anchor placement gate:** State the one-sentence anchor in the answer or first core paragraph. A later strong section cannot fully rescue a generic first screen.
3. **Detail-reason gate:** For every major scenario, write one sentence explaining why the detail changes the interpretation. Association lists without a change mechanism score no higher than serviceable.
4. **Neighbor-visibility gate:** Name the closest neighbor in the page journey and state what it covers that this page does not. This is required when collision evidence is non-trivial.

These changes improve measurement; they do not require every page to have the same number of sections or scenarios.

## Model pages after calibration

- **Tsunami:** model for natural-disaster pages because it connects scale, resources, response, and outcome without prediction.
- **Marrying a Celebrity:** model for romantic/identity pages because it interprets the represented quality and public/private tension rather than the celebrity noun.
- **11:11:** model for spiritual/numerological pages because it preserves spiritual interest while separating tradition, association, pattern recognition, and evidence.
- **Saying No:** model for boundary/choice pages because it asks what the refusal protects and lets the emotional aftermath do the interpretive work.

Crows and Eyes remain useful references after their targeted first-screen revisions, but should not yet be the category exemplars.
